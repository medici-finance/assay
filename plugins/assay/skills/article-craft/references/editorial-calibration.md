# Calibrate an editorial rule

Choose comparison pieces aimed at the same reader, genre and decision. For each, mark a specific passage and describe what it enables the reader to understand or do. Separate factual and learning defects from preferences about tone, length and heading style.

Test a disputed rule against a strong passage and a plausible weak one. Suppose a study followed twelve libraries using a voluntary reporting system. The sentence “Eight of the twelve participating libraries reported shorter waits” preserves both the population and the source of the observation. “Libraries cut waiting times by adopting the system” extends the claim to other libraries and introduces causation. A useful rule accepts the first and asks what evidence supports the second.

Record the original rule, the two passages, the judgments and the proposed change. Reapply the change to both passages. If it rewards extra caveats while the unsupported conclusion survives, revise the rule or retire it. Use a passage and its consequence to explain the judgment rather than assigning a single quality score.

## Article review rubric

Read the assignment and the finished article in a separate review pass. For each applicable criterion, quote the shortest passage that supports your judgment and explain its effect on the intended reader. If you need a source, a voice sample or a fresh reader to decide, record what remains unverified. For an excerpt, limit the judgment to what the excerpt shows.

| Criterion | Question to ask | Evidence to cite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Argument | What does the article promise, and does it earn its conclusion? | Quote the central claim and point to the example or reasoning that supports the move from claim to conclusion. |
| Evidence | Can a reader trace the claims carrying the conclusion to their sources? | Check qualitative and causal claims as well as numbers. For a measurement, name the population, comparison and conditions. Distinguish reported results, independent checks and illustrative examples. |
| Honesty about limits | Which limitation would change what a reader believes or does? | Quote the qualification beside the claim it limits. Check whether the article distinguishes proposed, implemented and released behavior where the difference matters. |
| Voice | Does the wording serve this reader and match the supplied voice guidance? | Quote a sentence whose rhythm, diction or technical precision helps or obstructs understanding. Use a writing sample for claims about an individual author’s voice. |
| Genre fit | Does the article do the job of its chosen genre? | Compare its structure with the [genre guide](genres.md). For a tutorial, identify the runnable step and expected result. For an explainer, identify the passage that makes the mechanism understandable. |
| Audience | Can someone with the reader’s assumed knowledge use what the article teaches? | Name the reader’s concern and promised outcome. Cite the passage that enables the next action and identify any prerequisite the reader would have to supply. |

To test the audience criterion, give a fresh reader the article and audience brief. Ask them to explain the central idea, cite what taught it, and apply it when one input changes. For a recommendation, ask what evidence would change their choice. Label a simulated reader or an author self-check as such. A successful retelling measures comprehension and still leaves factual claims to be checked against sources.

Close the review by naming the weakest criterion and the consequence for the reader, even when the article meets the brief. Keep the passage-level findings visible. A single overall score loses the distinction between a source problem and a style preference.
