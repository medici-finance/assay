# Develop the film before polishing it

Use this with the shared video editorial review and video-author. It guides
creative work; it adds no permission gate or mandatory document. Keep decisions,
sketches and findings in the existing `editorial-review.md` and `motion-spec.md`.
Scale the exploration to the job: a small repair should remain a small repair.

## Find a visual idea, not a template

Start from the audience's question, evidence and intended change in understanding.
Ask what the viewer should notice, expect and discover. Develop genuinely different
ways to show that discovery before committing to detailed animation. For a new
film or substantial redesign, three quick treatments are a useful starting point,
not a quota. Vary the explanatory device, framing or sequence, not just the colours.
A sketch, a few stills or a short rough sequence is enough to compare them.

For each promising treatment, note:
- the recognizable subject and what changes about it;
- the visual discovery: what the image makes understandable beyond spoken labels;
- how the metaphor maps to the real subject, and where it breaks down;
- the strongest alternative and why this choice suits this audience and evidence.

Choose using explanatory power, distinctiveness, continuity and feasibility. An
unusual treatment that implies an unsupported result loses to a truthful one.
Borrow techniques from references, not another film's assets or identity. Record
the passage or technique actually inspected and the medium used; a still cannot
establish how sound and motion work together.

## Let script and images develop together

Stage the audience, evidence and provisional story first. Develop rough visuals
alongside the script, then revise both when a better explanation emerges. Staging
still owns claims and order; update its contract and re-review affected evidence
and comprehension before detailed production. Never silently change approved
content, invent an event, or substitute visual polish for a missing explanation.
Concrete treatment choices belong in the editorial review, not `staging.yaml`.

Think in shots as well as pipeline scenes. One narration row can contain a close-up,
a wider reveal and a new viewpoint. Follow the viewer's eye across those changes:
preserve a useful anchor, anticipate where attention goes next, and leave time to
inspect the discovery. Scale, negative space, juxtaposition, perspective, repetition
and stillness can all do explanatory work. Typography can be the subject instead
of a label pasted above a diagram. No technique is compulsory.

## Test rhythm cheaply

Build an animatic before refining every move. Use an existing approved take or
explicitly authorized temporary voice when available; mark temporary audio and
its rights/source. Choose sound for a job: emphasis, continuity, atmosphere or a
useful pause. Silence is also a choice. Do not choose a final voice, run production
TTS/encoding, acquire assets or publish outside the existing authorization boundary.
If no usable audio exists, proceed with a clearly labelled silent timing study;
voice alignment and audiovisual rhythm remain not-yet-checked.

Watch the rough sequence at speed. Does each reveal arrive when the viewer can
understand it? Is there a surprise worth waiting for? Does the sequence have
contrast in pace and scale? Does a long hold permit thought or merely fill time?
Adjust the shot/script relationship now; exact motion curves come afterwards.
Select a coherent motion language for detailed work and refine it deliberately
as playback reveals problems. Keep the implementation deterministic and retain
the renderer, seam, duration, accessibility and evidence checks.

## Critique the actual result

In the existing review, name the strongest visual discovery, weakest passage,
repetitive composition and most tempting false inference. State what the image
adds to the voice. Inspect stills for composition and playback for continuity,
attention and rhythm; check the actual voice and captions before calling the film
ready. Test reduced-motion playback and the intended viewing size.

Retain a small before/after specimen with the reason the change helps; do not
turn that specimen's aesthetic into a rule for the next film. If all alternatives
look like the same card layout, return to the visual idea before adding effects.
A test PASS confirms its stated contract, not creativity or audience understanding.

## Worked comparison: abundant output, scarce judgment

A restrained treatment can keep one work item beside labels, multiply copies,
then stop the item at a judgment boundary. A more spatial treatment can pull
back from the same item to reveal a field of work, return to inspection scale,
and turn the author's emphatic answer into one viewpoint beside the unresolved
judgment. Both can preserve the same narration. The latter adds a visual discovery;
it also needs checks that perspective does not suggest a hidden defect and that
the illustrative population is not mistaken for a measured output count.

This is an example of changing the explanatory device, not a required paper,
camera or perspective style. Compare the actual sequence and intended audience.
A caption-only correction to either treatment does not reopen concept exploration.

## Research basis and limits — October 2026

These are production practices adapted in house words, not validated universal
thresholds or evidence of measured gains in our videos:

- [Ordinary Folk's process](https://www.ordinaryfolk.co/process): explore design
  and movement before final animation, with sound considered during development.
- [Disney Animation: Story](https://www.disneyanimation.com/process/story/) and
  [Editorial & Sound](https://disneyanimation.com/process/editorial--sound/):
  develop framing and timing through boards, editing and temporary sound.
- [School of Motion: animatics](https://schoolofmotion.com/blog/what-are-animatics-and-why-are-they-important)
  and [eye tracing](https://schoolofmotion.com/blog/eye-tracing-animation): test
  the sequence cheaply and direct attention through cuts.
- [Animation Mentor: anticipation](https://www.animationmentor.com/blog/anticipation-the-12-basic-principles-of-animation/)
  and [exaggeration](https://www.animationmentor.com/blog/exaggeration-the-12-basic-principles-of-animation/):
  purposeful preparation and emphasis can clarify an action. They do not license
  exaggerating evidence, numerical results or product capability.
